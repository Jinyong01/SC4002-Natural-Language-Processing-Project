You: Would like to check, why would freezing the Glove embedding be better? Also what is character label encoder and how will that help our current model? Would randomly replacing words with unk may possibly worsen the performance?
-----------------------
Claude: Short answers: freezing GloVe isn't automatically better, it only fixes one specific problem. A character-level encoder builds a word's vector from its spelling. And yes, word dropout can hurt if you overdo it, but your current setup has a gap it fills.

1. Why freeze the GloVe embeddings?
The problem it solves. Say you add dev/test words that GloVe knows to the vocabulary. Those words never appear in train, so their embedding rows never get a gradient. They stay exactly as raw GloVe.

Meanwhile, the embeddings of train words move away from GloVe during training. The BiLSTM learns to read those moved vectors. At test time, an unseen word comes in with an original GloVe vector, while its neighbours (for example "song" and "track") have drifted. The model reads the two kinds of vector differently, so much of the benefit of adding the word is lost.

Freezing keeps every word in the same, unchanged GloVe space. The model learns to read GloVe itself, so a word it never saw in training still makes sense to it.

The cost. The embeddings can no longer adapt to your task. That matters here because GloVe puts opposites close together: on/off, up/down, increase/decrease, like/dislike. Those pairs decide intents like iot_hue_lighton vs iot_hue_lightoff or audio_volume_up vs audio_volume_down. Fine-tuning can push them apart; freezing can't.

Middle-ground options:

Frozen GloVe plus one shared trainable linear layer on top. Every word, seen or unseen, goes through the same learned transformation, so unseen words get adapted too. This is usually the best compromise.
Freeze for the first few epochs, then unfreeze with a lower learning rate.
Keep trainable vectors for the 384 train words GloVe doesn't have. They have no GloVe vector, so they need one learned from scratch either way.
Which wins is an empirical question, so try them on dev.

2. What a character-level encoder is and how it helps
Right now each word is one id, looked up in a table. A character encoder also builds a vector from the word's letters:


"wednesdays" → w e d n e s d a y s → char embeddings (≈30-dim each)
             → 1-D CNN + max-pool (or a small BiLSTM) → char vector (≈50–100-dim)
final word vector = [GloVe vector ; char vector] → BiLSTM (as now)
It adds very few parameters (well under 100K), so the 20M limit isn't an issue.

How it helps your model:

Words neither train nor GloVe knows. From my count, that's about 47 words in dev and 95 in test. Today they all become the same <unk>.
Word forms and spelling variants. It can link wednesdays to wednesday, alarms to alarm, tomorrow's to tomorrow, and handle typos.
Word shape, which matters for slots. Digits and patterns like "7am", "5:30" or "o'clock" (time), "@" and ".com" (email_address), and number-like words (change_amount) all get useful signals.
Downsides: slightly slower training, a small extra overfitting risk, and the gain is mostly on rare or unseen words. Expect it to help slot F1 more than intent accuracy.

3. Could randomly replacing words with <unk> hurt?
Yes, if done carelessly. But your current setup has a problem that makes it worth trying:

With MIN_FREQ = 1, no training word ever maps to <unk>. Its embedding is never updated and stays at its random starting value. At test time, the roughly 3.5% of unknown words are fed a random vector the model has never learned to read.

Word dropout fixes this. During training only, each word is swapped for <unk> with a small probability. This teaches the <unk> vector to mean "some unknown word", so the model learns to use the surrounding words instead.

How it can hurt:

Too high a rate destroys the signal. MASSIVE has many very short utterances ("quiet", "olly quiet"). Replacing the key word leaves an example that can't be labelled correctly, which is just noise.
It removes slot words. If "james" becomes <unk>, the model still has to tag it B-person. That's useful practice in moderation, but too much makes the tagger guess from context alone.
It can be unnecessary. If you add GloVe words to the vocabulary and add a char encoder, true <unk> cases become rare, and the gain shrinks to ordinary regularization.
How to use it safely:

Use a small rate, about 0.05–0.1, and tune it on dev.
Replace rare words more often than common ones. A common rule is to replace a word with probability α / (α + its train count). Rare words are the ones most likely to be unknown at test time.
Never replace every word in an utterance, and turn it off at evaluation time.
If you add a char encoder, replace only the word embedding and keep the characters. The model then learns to fall back on spelling when it doesn't know a word, which is exactly the situation it faces at test time.

You: Would like to check what is CRF layer and how will that help our current model?
-----------------------
Claude: A CRF (conditional random field) layer sits on top of your slot output and chooses the whole tag sequence at once, instead of each word's tag on its own. It learns which tags may follow which, so it stops the model producing invalid or inconsistent tag sequences.

What your model does now
In JointBiLSTM, the slot_head gives each word a score for each of the 111 tags. predict() then takes the highest-scoring tag for each word separately:


word:     wake  me  up  at   five    am
argmax:   O     O   O   O   B-time  I-date   ← each word decided alone
Each choice is reasonable by itself, but together they make an invalid sequence: I-date cannot follow B-time. Your decoder handles this by treating the stray I-date as a new slot. You get two wrong slots (time: five and date: am) instead of one correct time: five am. Because the scorer needs exact matches, that costs one false negative and two false positives.

The model has no way to say "the previous word was B-time, so this one is probably I-time".

What a CRF adds
1. A transition matrix. This is a learned 111 × 111 table of scores for "tag A followed by tag B", about 12K parameters. After training it learns, for example:

B-time → I-time: high score
B-time → I-date: very negative
O → I-anything: very negative
2. Scoring whole sequences. The score of a tag sequence is the sum of each word's own tag score (what your slot_head already outputs) plus the transition score between every pair of neighbouring tags.

3. Training. The slot cross-entropy loss is replaced by the CRF loss. It raises the score of the correct full sequence relative to all possible sequences, computed efficiently with the forward algorithm. The intent loss stays as it is.

4. Prediction. Instead of argmax per word, the Viterbi algorithm finds the single highest-scoring sequence. In the example above, B-time I-time wins because the transition B-time → I-date is heavily penalised:


CRF:      O     O   O   O   B-time  I-time   ✓  → time: five am
How it helps your model
No invalid BIO sequences. Slots are always whole, never broken into pieces.
Consistent types within a slot. A slot can't switch type halfway through.
Better boundaries. The tag choices around a slot's edges become linked. This matters a lot under exact-match scoring, where being off by one word scores zero.
It may lift precision. Your precision is below recall (dev 76.3 / 78.4), which suggests extra or broken slots, the kind of error a CRF prevents.
Limitations
The gain is usually modest. On BiLSTM taggers it's often about 1–2 F1, because the BiLSTM already sees context in both directions. Your error analysis would show how many points are really available: count the invalid sequences and boundary errors.
It doesn't fix the other weaknesses. Unknown words, wrong slot types and rare classes stay as they are; those need the vocabulary, char-encoder and class-weighting fixes.
Training is somewhat slower, because the CRF loss and Viterbi decoding have to go through the sequence step by step.
A cheaper first test: constrained decoding
You can get much of the "no invalid sequences" benefit without retraining. Keep your current model and run Viterbi with a fixed transition table that forbids invalid moves (O → I-x, B-x → I-y, I-x → I-y) and allows everything else. If this alone improves dev F1, a trained CRF will probably improve it further. If it barely changes anything, the CRF is unlikely to be worth the effort.

Fitting it into your code
Use the pytorch-crf package (from torchcrf import CRF), or write it yourself; it's about 60 lines.
Pass attention_mask as the CRF mask, so padding is skipped. The CRF needs real tag ids at padded positions, so replace the -100 padding labels with 0 there.
Replace slot_loss_fn(...) with the CRF's negative log-likelihood, and slot_logits.argmax(-1) in predict() with crf.decode(...).

You: can you clarify point 4 regarding the 'feed an intent signal' and the 'add scenario prediction'
-----------------------
Claude: These are two separate ideas. Both try to make the model share more information between its outputs, but in different ways:

Feeding an intent signal helps the slot tagger by telling it what the utterance is about.
Scenario prediction helps the intent classifier by giving it an easier, coarser task to learn alongside.
A. Feeding an intent signal into the slot tagger
The problem now
In JointBiLSTM, both outputs read from the same BiLSTM, but they work in parallel:


BiLSTM output ──► slot_head   → slot tags
              └─► max-pool → intent_head → intent
The slot tagger never sees what the intent is. Yet the intent strongly limits which slot types make sense:

Utterance	Intent	Correct slot
play jazz	play_music	music_genre
play jazz fm	play_radio	radio_name
set alarm for five	alarm_set	time
what's on my calendar friday	calendar_query	date
When the tagger finds a slot but gives it the wrong type, the missing piece is often "what is this utterance about?". The intent answers exactly that.

How to do it
Simple version: concatenation. Turn the intent prediction into a vector and attach it to every word's BiLSTM output before the slot head

Fancier version: slot-gated model (Goo et al., 2018). Instead of attaching the vector, a learned gate uses the intent to decide how much each word's features should count for slot tagging. It's the same idea with more parameters; try the simple version first.

Points to watch
Use the predicted intent probabilities, not the correct intent label. At test time only the prediction exists. If you train with the correct label, the tagger learns to trust a signal that is always right in training but wrong about 15% of the time at test.
Errors can spread. If the intent is wrong, the slot types may now be wrong too. Probabilities soften this: if the model is unsure between play_music and play_radio, both keep weight.
It mainly fixes wrong-type errors, not boundary errors. Your error analysis shows whether wrong-type errors are a big share of the slot errors.
B. Adding scenario prediction as an extra training task
What "scenario" is
Every MASSIVE record has a scenario field (18 values: alarm, audio, iot, music, cooking, …). It's a coarse group of intents, usually visible in the intent name

How to do it
Add a third output head next to the intent head, used only during training:
The model's input is still only the words. The scenario is just an extra label it learns to predict during training, so nothing changes at test time. Its prediction can simply be ignored, or used for the consistency check below.

Why it can help
Rare intents borrow from related ones. cooking_query has only 4 training examples. On its own, the intent head barely learns it. The scenario head learns "this is about cooking" from all cooking utterances, and the shared encoder carries that over to the rare intent.
An easier task to learn first. Telling apart 18 broad topics is easier than 60 fine-grained intents. That gives the encoder a cleaner basis to work from, and also acts as mild regularization.
Optional consistency check. At prediction time, you can only allow intents that belong to the predicted scenario, or add each intent's score to its scenario's score. This rules out nonsensical predictions such as alarm_set for a cooking question.
Limitations
It adds no new information. The scenario follows directly from the intent label, so it reorganises the training signal rather than adding to it. Expect a small gain (about 0–1 point), mostly on rare intents.
It doesn't help confusions within one scenario, such as iot_hue_lighton vs iot_hue_lightoff, because both are iot. Your intent confusion matrix shows which kind dominates. If most intent errors stay inside one scenario, this idea won't help much.
Check it's allowed. Your notebook states the scenario field isn't used. Using it only as a training label is usually fine, but confirm with your group and the assignment brief before relying on it.
How they fit together
A (intent → slots) mostly targets slot errors of the wrong type.
B (scenario head) mostly targets intent errors on rare or cross-scenario cases.

You: Would like to check with point 5 'class-weighted or focal loss, simple data augmentation' what does this means?
-----------------------
Claude: These are three ways to deal with class imbalance: some labels have hundreds of training examples, others only a handful. Your data is very uneven:

Slot types: 19 of 55 have fewer than 50 training examples. music_album and game_type have 1 each, sport_type 5, audiobook_author 6.
Intents: calendar_set has 810 examples, cooking_query has 4.
Tags: most words are O, and 33% of training utterances have no slots at all.
With plain cross-entropy, every training word counts the same. The loss is dominated by O and the common classes, so the model has little reason to learn rare ones. The three techniques below fix this from different angles.

1. Class-weighted loss
Idea: give mistakes on rare classes a bigger penalty, so the model pays more attention to them.

Each class gets a weight that is larger when the class is rarer, usually something like 1 / count, often softened with a square root
Slot tags work the same way: count each of the 111 tags in tag_ids and pass the weights to slot_loss_fn. This also lowers the weight of O.

Risks:

It can lower your overall score. Micro-F1 is dominated by frequent slots like date, time and place_name. Pushing the model toward rare classes can add extra slots and cost precision on the common ones. The square root above, or a cap on the weights, keeps this in check.
Very rare classes get extreme weights. With 1 example, music_album would get a huge weight, and the model would overfit to that single sentence. Cap the weights, for example at 10×.
Plain weights don't fit a CRF. The CRF loss scores the whole sequence, so it has no per-class weight setting.
2. Focal loss
Idea: instead of weighting by class, weight by how hard each example is. Words the model already gets right with high confidence contribute almost nothing; words it gets wrong or is unsure about dominate the loss.

Focal loss multiplies cross-entropy by (1 − p)^γ, where p is the model's probability for the correct class:

p (confidence in the correct tag)	(1 − p)² with γ = 2	Effect
0.99 (easy O)	0.0001	ignored
0.6	0.16	reduced
0.1 (hard, rare slot)	0.81	almost full loss

Why it fits your data: the thousands of easy O words stop swamping the loss, without you choosing weights by hand. Rare classes tend to be the hard ones, so they get more attention automatically.

Risks: γ needs tuning (try 1 to 2). Mislabelled or truly ambiguous examples always stay "hard", so the model can over-focus on them. Like class weights, focal loss doesn't fit a CRF directly.

3. Simple data augmentation
Idea: create extra training examples for rare classes by changing existing ones.

Slot value swapping is the most natural method for your data. Take a training utterance and replace a slot's words with another value of the same slot type seen elsewhere in train
The intent and the BIO tags follow automatically: the new words get B-song_name I-song_name. Apply it mostly to utterances that contain rare slot types or rare intents, so they get extra copies.

Other simple options:

Oversampling: repeat utterances from rare intents or slot types more often in each epoch. It's the easiest option, but the model sees the exact same sentences again, so it can overfit.
Word dropout (from earlier) is also a kind of augmentation.
Risks:

Unnatural sentences. Swapping a value can produce odd combinations, such as an artist paired with the wrong song. That's usually harmless, but don't swap across slot types.
It can't create real variety for 1-example classes. With one music_album example, swapping gives new album names in the same sentence. The value changes, but the model still learns only one way of phrasing it.
Keep it to train. Augment only the training data, using only values from train. Never touch dev or test. Your notebook says the data is used exactly as released, so check that the brief allows train-side augmentation.
